The claim
"AI data centers are destroying the environment and driving up everyone's electric bills."
Misleading Real growth, missing context on efficiency gains and who actually pays.

What's true

Data center electricity consumption is growing. The IEA projects global data center demand will roughly double between 2022 and 2026. That is a real number and worth taking seriously.

What the data actually shows

The efficiency story is the one that gets left out. Energy use per computation has fallen by a factor of roughly 1,000 over the past decade — meaning today's AI does vastly more per watt than the systems of 2014. The absolute consumption grows because the volume of computation grows faster than efficiency improves, not because the technology is becoming less efficient.

On household bills: data centers negotiate directly with utilities at wholesale rates and sign long-term power purchase agreements. Residential ratepayers are on a completely different tariff structure. The transmission and distribution infrastructure costs that do affect bills are spread across all commercial and industrial consumers, of which data centers are one category among thousands.

The bigger picture

The same AI infrastructure accused of consuming power is also the primary tool being used to optimize renewable energy grids, predict solar and wind output, reduce industrial energy waste, and accelerate materials science for next-generation batteries. The energy calculus is far more complicated than the headline suggests.

The claim
"AI will eliminate most jobs within a decade. Mass unemployment is inevitable."
Misleading Automation changes jobs far more often than it eliminates them — and always has.

What's true

AI is automating specific tasks within jobs, and some roles will shrink or shift. Radiologists reading routine scans, paralegals doing document review, and coders writing boilerplate are all seeing real automation pressure. That's real and worth preparing for.

What the data actually shows

The countries with the highest rates of industrial automation — Germany, Japan, South Korea, Singapore — consistently have among the lowest unemployment rates in the world. Automation compresses the cost of goods and services, which increases purchasing power, which creates demand for new categories of work that didn't exist before.

The ATM is the canonical example: it was supposed to eliminate bank tellers. Instead, it reduced the cost per branch so much that banks opened more branches, and the total number of tellers grew for two decades after ATM rollout. The pattern repeats across every major wave of automation in recorded economic history.

The bigger picture

The economic risk of AI is not mass unemployment — it is transition speed. Some workers will need to retrain faster than previous generations did. That's a real policy challenge. It is a very different problem from the apocalyptic framing that dominates headlines, and it has a very different set of solutions.

The claim
"AI is hitting a wall. Scaling is dead. The LLM era is over."
Busted This claim has been made and disproven repeatedly since 2022.

What's true

Pre-training scaling returns are becoming more expensive to achieve. Getting the next doubling of effective capability requires more compute than the previous doubling. That is a real observation about the economics of frontier model development.

What the data actually shows

"Scaling is dead" has been confidently declared after GPT-3, after GPT-4, after Claude 2, and after Gemini 1.0. Each time, the next generation of models arrived and the claim was quietly forgotten. The reason is that the original claim confuses one scaling axis slowing down with all paths to capability improvement closing.

Post-training techniques — RLHF, constitutional AI, reasoning chains, tool use, and agentic scaffolding — have delivered capability jumps entirely separate from raw pre-training scale. Inference-time compute scaling (thinking longer, not just training bigger) is an entirely new axis that was barely explored before 2024 and is now central to frontier lab roadmaps. Andrej Karpathy joined Anthropic in May 2026 specifically to work on pre-training acceleration using AI-assisted research loops — the opposite of a field running out of runway.

4×
Times "scaling is dead" was declared since GPT-3 — and reversed
3+
Independent scaling axes now active: pre-train, post-train, inference-time

The bigger picture

The field is not hitting a wall. It is discovering that the wall it thought existed was actually a door. Every "end of scaling" moment in AI history has preceded a new approach that unlocked the next phase of progress. There is no strong theoretical reason to believe the current moment is different.

The claim
"AI data centers are drinking our water supply dry."
Partial Water use is real and improving rapidly. The comparison context is almost always missing.

What's true

Data centers that use evaporative cooling do consume water. A large hyperscale facility can use millions of gallons per year. That is a real number that deserves real scrutiny, especially in water-stressed regions.

What the data actually shows

The missing comparison: a single 18-hole golf course in the American Southwest uses approximately 90 million gallons of water per year. The United States has roughly 16,000 golf courses. Agriculture accounts for 70% of global freshwater withdrawals. Data centers, in aggregate, account for less than 0.5% of global water withdrawals.

More importantly, the industry is actively moving away from water cooling. Microsoft, Google, and Meta have all announced or deployed closed-loop liquid cooling systems that use zero evaporative water loss. Microsoft's next-generation data centers are designed to achieve net-zero water use in operations. The trajectory is sharply in the right direction.

70%

0

The bigger picture

Water stewardship matters and the industry should be held to account on it. The trajectory of the technology is toward water-free cooling, not more consumption. The current moment of water use is a transition artifact, not a permanent condition — and it is orders of magnitude smaller than the sectors that never make headlines for their water consumption.

The claim
"AI in medicine is dangerous. You can't trust a machine with your health."
Busted FDA-cleared AI diagnostics are outperforming human baselines in multiple specialties.

What's true

Early AI medical tools had real problems — overfitting to training populations, poor generalization across demographics, and a lack of interpretability. Those were legitimate concerns that the field has spent years addressing.

What the data actually shows

The FDA has cleared over 900 AI-enabled medical devices as of 2025, the majority in radiology. Multiple peer-reviewed studies show AI diagnostic tools matching or exceeding specialist performance in specific tasks: diabetic retinopathy screening, breast cancer detection on mammography, skin lesion classification, and cardiac arrhythmia detection on ECG.

The key framing is AI as augmentation, not replacement. The most effective deployments pair AI with physicians — the AI catches what the human misses and the human catches what the AI misses. Error rates drop dramatically versus either working alone. This is not a hypothetical; it is the clinical evidence from deployed systems.

The bigger picture

The question is not whether to trust machines with health decisions — physicians already use machines constantly (MRI, CT, ECG, lab analyzers). The question is whether AI tools meet the evidentiary bar for clinical use. For a growing number of applications, peer-reviewed evidence and regulatory clearance say they do.

The claim
"Quantum computers will break all encryption and destroy internet security overnight."
Misleading Cryptographically relevant quantum computers are years to decades away — and post-quantum encryption is already deploying.

What's true

A sufficiently powerful quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could theoretically break RSA and ECC encryption — the backbone of most current internet security. This is a real theoretical threat and the cryptography community has taken it seriously for over two decades.

What the data actually shows

Breaking 2048-bit RSA encryption requires an estimated 4,000+ logical qubits running with very low error rates. The best quantum computers in 2026 have hundreds to low thousands of physical qubits with error rates that require thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit. We are likely a decade or more from cryptographically relevant scale — and that estimate has not moved materially in five years.

More importantly, NIST finalized its first post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024. Major browsers, cloud providers, and government agencies are already deploying quantum-resistant encryption. The security community is not waiting — the transition is underway now, well before any threat materializes.

The bigger picture

Quantum computing is genuinely exciting and the progress is real — but the most valuable near-term applications are optimization, simulation, and drug discovery, not breaking encryption. The encryption threat is real in theory, well understood, and being actively mitigated. The "overnight collapse of internet security" framing ignores twenty years of preparation by the cryptography community.

The claim
"AI models just plagiarize and remix existing work. There's nothing creative or intelligent happening."
Busted This fundamentally misunderstands how large language models generate output.

What's true

LLMs are trained on human-generated text and their outputs are statistically shaped by that training data. Memorization of specific training examples does occur and is a legitimate area of ongoing research and mitigation.

What the data actually shows

The claim conflates training on data with copying data — a fundamental technical error. When a human reads thousands of books and then writes a novel, we do not say they plagiarized every book they read. LLMs learn statistical relationships between concepts, not a lookup table of sentences to retrieve.

More concretely: frontier AI models are solving novel mathematical proofs, designing new protein structures that have never existed in nature, synthesizing research across disciplines to generate genuinely new hypotheses, and writing code for problems with no prior solution in the training data. AlphaFold solved the protein folding problem — a 50-year grand challenge of biology — by generating structural predictions for proteins never before characterized. That is not remix. That is discovery.

200M+

The bigger picture

The debate about AI creativity is genuinely interesting and philosophically rich. The claim that AI is "just plagiarism" is not that debate — it is a technical mischaracterization that falls apart on contact with what frontier models are actually doing in science, mathematics, and engineering.
